01 · Positioning

From idea to object.

EverHaven's structural risk is reading as two companies — a product shop and a job shop. The fix is to position the capability as the brand, and both audiences as customers of that capability. Whether someone buys a finished product or commissions a prototype, they are buying the same thing: EverHaven's ability to take an idea through design, engineering, and manufacturing to a finished object.

Positioning statement: EverHaven is a product design and manufacturing company. The shop is proof of the capability; the services are access to it. Every product page quietly sells the services ("designed and made by EverHaven"), and every service page quietly sells the products ("see what we make for ourselves").

Core value proposition

"We design, engineer, and manufacture original products — and we'll do the same for yours."

One sentence answers "what do you do," proves trust (our own products exist), and sets up both paths. Working copy — final headline wording comes with the homepage design.

02 · Messaging hierarchy

What every page says, in order.

L1
Capability

We take ideas to finished objects — design, engineering, manufacturing. The umbrella message; owns the homepage hero and the About story.

L2
Proof

Our own products, our process, our equipment and materials. Owns the Shop, Capabilities, and Featured Projects. No claims we can't show.

L3
Partnership

How working with us feels: responsive, precise, collaborative. Owns Services pages, How It Works, and the quote experience.

L4
Action

Two verbs only, everywhere: Shop products and Get a quote. Every page ends at one of the two. "Get a quote" is always the visually dominant CTA.

Voice: studio "we," sentence case, concrete verbs, no hype adjectives, no exclamation marks. Confident enough to use short declarative headlines; approachable enough to explain jargon the first time it appears.

03 · Customer journeys

Two paths, one front door.

Path A · Shopper

Home → Choose-your-path card → Shop → Collection → Product → Cart → Checkout.

Shortcuts: featured products on Home link straight to product pages; STL buyers get a dedicated digital collection with instant-download framing. Exit ramps into Path B on every product page ("want this customized? Get a quote").

Path B · Project client

Home → Choose-your-path card → Services (or straight to Get a quote) → Service detail → Capabilities (for validation) → Quote form → human follow-up.

Trust is built in three beats before the form: what we do → how we do it → what it's like to work with us. The quote form itself stays short; detail gathering happens in follow-up.

Undecided visitors (need a replacement part, have a sketch, not sure which service) get a third, quieter route: a "Not sure where to start?" link under the path cards that leads directly to the quote form with a low-pressure framing — describing the problem is enough.

05 · Navigation

Six items, one dominant action.

Desktop

Sticky 64–72px header on the canvas color, hairline bottom border. Left: EverHaven wordmark. Center/right: Home is the logo; then Shop · Services · Capabilities · About as text links; then cart icon; then Get a quote as the only filled button in the header. Shop and Services get simple single-panel dropdowns listing their children — no mega-menu until the catalog earns it. Announcement bar above the header (dark, one line) reserved for a real message, e.g. the quote CTA — never a fake urgency banner.

Mobile

Header keeps three things: menu button, wordmark, cart. Get a quote moves to a persistent full-width bar pinned above the fold inside the drawer AND appears as the first item when the menu opens. The drawer is a full-screen sheet: five primary links at large tap size (56px rows), children as indented accordions, quote button fixed at the bottom of the sheet. No hamburger-hidden CTA — the quote action is never more than one tap deep.

06 · Homepage content strategy

Your eleven beats, tightened to nine.

Your brief's homepage story is right; I recommend two merges so the page doesn't sprawl: fold Capabilities preview into the Services band (services say what, capabilities strip says with-what), and fold Industries & Applications into Featured Projects (applications shown through real objects beat an abstract industries grid — and we can't name industries served yet anyway).

1Hero — capability statement + both CTAs + one strong workshop/product image or a quiet build-process artifact. No carousel.
2Choose your path — two large cards (Shop / Commission a project) + the quiet "not sure?" third route.
3Featured products — 3–4 products max, editorially framed ("designed and made by EverHaven"), link to Shop.
4Services + capabilities strip — six service cards; beneath them a single-row strip of materials/processes acting as the Capabilities teaser.
5How it works — numbered 01–04 process on a dark band: describe → quote → make → deliver.
6Featured projects / applications — real objects when they exist; clearly-labeled placeholder tiles until then. Section hides cleanly if empty.
7About preview — the company story in three sentences + link. Founder appears here as a human note, not the headline.
8FAQ — 5–6 genuine questions (files, materials, quantities, shipping, STL licensing). Accordion.
9Final CTA — dark closing band, single purpose: Get a quote, with a lightweight inline form variant considered in Phase 2.
07 · Shop architecture

A shop that never looks thin.

The reference's weakest pages were collections exposed with one or two products. EverHaven's shop is designed around density-adaptive layouts:

  • Shop landing = collection storefront, not a grid. It opens with an editorial header, then collection cards (Physical / Digital STL / by-use collections), then a "new + best" strip. It looks complete with 6 products or 600.
  • Collections switch layout by count: 1–3 products render as large editorial feature rows (image left, story right); 4+ switch to the standard 3-up grid. No orphaned single-card grids, no empty placeholder tiles.
  • Digital STL files are first-class: a templated branded cover-art system (like the reference's guide covers) so digital listings look owned; product pages state license terms and print settings plainly.
  • Every product page cross-sells the capability: a standard "Made by EverHaven" block linking to services ("need it in a different material or size? Get a quote").
  • Collections that are empty don't exist publicly. Nav dropdowns and shop cards are driven by populated collections only.

Filtering/sorting stays minimal (availability, price, type) until the catalog exceeds ~20 items per collection.

08 · Services architecture

One template, six services.

A Services overview page frames the offer (who it's for, how engagement works) and routes to six detail pages. Every detail page uses the same proven formula, so adding a seventh service later is a content task:

Hero: eyebrow · headline · 2-line promise · [Get a quote] [See capabilities]
What this covers        → 3–6 concrete scope cards
How it works            → numbered 01–04 process (shared component)
What we need from you   → files / sketch / part — lowers the entry bar
Related capability      → materials & process strip relevant to this service
Closing CTA             → dark quote band with service pre-selected in the form

Tone: professional engineering services, priced by quote. No hobby framing, no invented tolerances or turnaround claims — scope cards describe what we do, not numbers we can't back yet.

10 · Quote experience

Short form, human follow-up.

The quote page is a 6/6 split: reassurance left (what happens after you submit, what makes a good brief, "a sketch is enough to start"), form right. The form launches with seven fields — name, email, project type (pre-selected when arriving from a service page), description, quantity, deadline (optional), file upload (optional, multiple) — plus a note field. Material preference joins in Phase 2 once the materials page exists to link against.

  • Friction control: only name, email, and description are required. Upload is drag-and-drop with format hints, never a gate.
  • Confidence: a "what happens next" 3-step strip directly under the submit button, and a stated response expectation once you can commit to one. NEEDS REAL INFO
  • Distribution: the same form appears embedded in the homepage closing band (Phase 2) and as the target of every "Get a quote" button, with context passed along.

12 · Color directions NEEDS APPROVAL

Three palettes, one to choose.

All three follow your brief's rule — neutral foundation, photography carries the color, one accent used only for actions. They differ in temperature and in what the accent says about the company.

A · Workshop warm

warm neutrals · copper accent
#FAF8F4 canvas
#F0EBE2 surface
#191817 ink/dark
#45423C graphite
#E5DFD4 hairline
#C2622D accent
#9E4E20 accent ↓

Reasoning: closest to your brief's "warm whites, charcoal, graphite." The copper accent evokes machined bronze and heated metal — craft with warmth. Most approachable of the three; photographs of wood, filament, and warm workshop light sit beautifully on it. Risk: warm cream + orange is currently common in maker/creator brands (including your reference) — differentiation must come from typography and photography discipline.

B · Graphite & signal

neutral-cool paper · safety-signal accent
#F7F6F3 canvas
#ECEAE5 surface
#16181A ink
#1A1D21 dark band
#3F4348 graphite
#D95B1E accent
#B04713 accent ↓

Reasoning: the accent references safety orange — the color of CNC e-stops, tooling, and CAD selection highlights. Reads unmistakably as engineering, not craft. The near-black dark band with a faint blue-steel cast gives Capabilities and How-it-works sections a precision-instrument feel. Risk: the coolest and most serious of the three; approachability has to come from copy voice and photography warmth.

C · Stone & verdigris

warm stone neutrals · deep teal accent
#FAFAF6 canvas
#EFEEE7 surface
#181A19 ink/dark
#4A4D49 graphite
#E3E1D8 hairline
#127469 accent
#0C574F accent ↓

Reasoning: the most differentiated. Deep teal against warm stone reads as premium lab / instrument-maker — nobody in the 3D-printing space owns this. Calm, timeless, and it grades beautifully against both raw-material photography and dark machine shots. Risk: teal carries less action-urgency on CTAs than orange; button prominence must come from size and placement.

My recommendation: B — Graphite & signal. It's the only palette that positions EverHaven as an engineering company first, which is the harder credibility to earn (the shop can be warm through photography regardless of palette). A is the safe warm option but sits closest to creator-brand territory; C is the boldest differentiator if you want to own an unusual color. All CTA pairings above pass WCAG AA on their canvas.

13 · Typography directions NEEDS APPROVAL

Three systems, set in their own type.

1 · Modern geometric sans

Space Grotesk + Schibsted Grotesk
From idea to object, made properly.

Body set in Schibsted Grotesk — open, contemporary, quietly friendly. Buttons and captions share the display face at small sizes.

Reads as: modern product company, design-startup energy. Strength: creative + innovative. Weakness: the least "manufacturing" of the three; Space Grotesk is widely used in web3/startup land and may age fastest.

2 · Technical / industrial sans

Archivo + IBM Plex Sans + IBM Plex Mono
From idea to object, made properly.

Body set in IBM Plex Sans — engineered, highly legible, warm enough for long reading. Spec labels, part numbers, and eyebrow captions set in IBM Plex Mono:

MATERIAL · PETG — LAYER 0.2 MM — QTY 250

Reads as: precision manufacturer with a design department. Strength: engineering credibility + timelessness; the mono layer gives the whole site a spec-sheet signature that doubles as a brand device. Weakness: needs the mono accents and generous spacing to avoid feeling plain.

3 · Restrained serif + sans

Source Serif 4 + Instrument Sans
From idea to object, made properly.

Body, UI, and buttons in Instrument Sans; the serif appears only at display sizes, never bold.

Reads as: established premium studio — the most "flagship headquarters" of the three. Strength: premium + timeless; ages best. Weakness: the least technical; engineers may read it as a brand agency rather than a manufacturer.

My recommendation: 2 — the technical system. It's the only direction that earns engineering credibility on sight, and the Plex Mono spec layer is an ownable brand device that works everywhere from capability tables to product packaging. Creativity and premium feel come from how it's used — scale contrast, whitespace, and photography — not from a decorative face. Pairs best with palette B; also works with A or C.

14 · Layout rules

Spacing, grid, radius, shadow, motion.

Grid

12-column, 1200px max content width, 24px gutters. Hero splits 6/6; feature grids 3-up; path cards 2-up; process 4-up. Full-bleed color bands with contained content.

Spacing

4px base scale: 4/8/12/16/24/32/48/64/96. Section rhythm 96px desktop, 56px mobile. Card interiors 32px. Generous by default — whitespace is the premium signal.

Radius

Hierarchical: 6px inputs/buttons, 10px cards, 14px hero artifacts, pill for badges. One family, never mixed within a component.

Elevation

Color-block first: depth comes from surface alternation (canvas → tinted card → dark band), never two same-tone bands adjacent. One shadow recipe only — 0 1px 3px at 8% ink — for overlays and hover states.

Motion

Fades and ≤12px translates. 200ms ease-out hover, 300ms ease-in-out layout. Scroll-triggered reveals used once per section, not per element. No parallax, no spring bounce. Respects prefers-reduced-motion.

Hover discipline

Buttons darken one step; links underline; cards gain the single shadow. Nothing scales or moves on hover.

15 · Photography & icons

One photographic standard.

  • Three shot types only: (1) macro detail — layer lines, threads, textures; (2) object-on-set — products on neutral seamless backgrounds, consistent angle and lighting; (3) environment — machines and hands at work, shallow depth of field.
  • Grade: consistent warm-neutral grade regardless of palette choice; no cool blue casts, no HDR, no b&w.
  • Never: stock offices, handshakes, laptops-with-charts, watermarked renders, or mixed-quality user photos beside studio shots.
  • Until real photography exists: designed placeholder frames labeled with the intended shot ("macro — nozzle detail"), so layouts are honest and the shot list doubles as your photo brief. NEEDS REAL INFO

Icons: a single open-source stroke set (Lucide), 1.5px stroke, 20/24px sizes, ink or muted color only — never the accent, so icons never compete with CTAs. No emoji anywhere.

16 · Component system

Eighteen components build every page.

Chrome      announcement bar · header/nav · footer · mobile drawer
Sections    hero (6/6) · path cards · section header (eyebrow+title+intro)
            icon-card grid · numbered process band · capability strip
            editorial feature row · dark CTA band · FAQ accordion
Commerce    product card · collection card · product gallery · cart line
Forms       quote form · input set · file dropzone · confirmation state

Every page in the sitemap is a composition of these — no page-specific one-offs. That's what keeps the site consistent as it grows and keeps the eventual Shopify build sane (each component maps to a section/block).

17 · Mobile behavior

Stack, never shrink.

  • All multi-column sections stack to single column; path cards and process steps become full-width swipeable or stacked cards, in reading order.
  • Display type scales from 56–64px desktop to 32–36px mobile; body never below 16px; tap targets 44px minimum.
  • Get a quote stays one tap away at all times (header button collapses to an icon+label chip, drawer pins it bottom).
  • Product grids go 2-up on mobile; editorial feature rows stack image-first.
  • Quote form becomes single-column with the reassurance content collapsed into an expandable "what happens next" above the form.
18 · Accessibility

AA as a floor.

  • All text/background pairs ≥ 4.5:1 (large display ≥ 3:1); accent-on-canvas CTA combinations verified per palette before approval.
  • Color never sole signal — links underline in body copy, form errors pair icon + text.
  • Full keyboard support: visible focus rings (2px accent outline), skip-to-content link, focus-trapped drawer and dialogs.
  • Semantic landmarks and heading order; accordion and drawer states announced via ARIA; form fields with real labels, not placeholders-as-labels.
  • Motion honors prefers-reduced-motion; no autoplaying video with sound; alt text standards written into the content guidelines.
19 · Shopify considerations

Designed to survive implementation.

  • Every component above maps to an Online Store 2.0 section with blocks — the design deliverables will name these mappings so the Dawn build is assembly, not interpretation.
  • Density-adaptive collections use collection product-count logic — achievable in Liquid without apps.
  • Digital STL delivery needs a digital-downloads app; quote form file uploads likely need a form app or custom endpoint — flagged as the two app dependencies.
  • Services, Capabilities, About, and Quote are pages/metaobject templates; navigation and announcement bar are theme settings.
  • Dawn's type/color settings can't express everything (mono spec layer, band alternation) — expect a moderate custom CSS layer, still theme-update-safe.
